Week 3 — Control Transformers

Sources: Class 3 PRE-slides, Amatrol Module 3 control-transformer lab

Lab target: wire the trainer control transformer and measure about 120 VAC across secondary terminals 2 and 5.

Key takeaways

  1. A control transformer steps voltage to a useful level, isolates the control circuit from the source, and creates a predictable measurement point for troubleshooting.
  2. Turns ratio (TR) = primary turns ÷ secondary turns; secondary voltage = primary voltage ÷ TR.
  3. The trainer's primary tap must match the supply voltage (208, 277, or 380 VAC); the lab uses the 208 VAC tap.
  4. Secondary windings connected in parallel give 120 VAC; connected in series they give 240 VAC. This lab needs parallel.
  5. Terminal 5 on the secondary is grounded, giving the control circuit a stable reference — expect ~120 VAC across 2–5, ~0 VAC from 5 to ground, and ~120 VAC from 2 to ground.

1. Vocabulary

  • Alternating current (AC): current that reverses direction periodically.
  • Volts alternating current (VAC): an AC voltage reading, such as 120 VAC.
  • Volt-amperes (VA): apparent power. VA = V × I.
  • National Electrical Code (NEC): U.S. electrical safety code used by industry.
  • Primary winding: transformer coil connected to the source.
  • Secondary winding: transformer coil that supplies the load.
  • Turns ratio (TR): primary coil turns ÷ secondary coil turns.

2. Why this topic matters

Most motor-control panels have a high-energy power circuit and a lower-energy control circuit. The transformer lets pushbuttons, selector switches, pilot lights, relays, and contactor coils use a standard control voltage.

When a control circuit does nothing, the first question is: do we actually have control voltage? A control transformer is a troubleshooting landmark — prove control power exists before blaming switches, coils, or lamps.

3. Power circuit vs. control circuit

Power circuitControl circuit
Feeds the motorDecides when the motor should start, stop, reverse, or signal status
Carries the current that produces mechanical motionDoes not usually carry motor current — controls the devices that switch motor current

Lab example: the selector switch controls a yellow lamp using transformer secondary voltage.

4. What a control transformer does

A control transformer uses magnetic coupling to transfer energy from a primary winding to a secondary winding. Three jobs:

  1. Step voltage to a useful control level.
  2. Provide isolation between source and control circuit.
  3. Create a predictable measurement point for troubleshooting.

Lab example: the Amatrol trainer steps 208 VAC line voltage down to about 120 VAC control voltage.

5. Magnetic coupling, not a direct wire

  • Primary winding — connected to the supply side.
  • Iron core — carries changing magnetic flux.
  • Secondary winding — supplies the control circuit.

The primary and secondary are magnetically coupled — they are not meant to be one continuous conductor. That separation (isolation) is a key reason control transformers are useful in control panels.

6. Step-down and step-up

Step-down transformerStep-up transformer
Secondary voltage is lower than primary voltageSecondary voltage is higher than primary voltage
Example: 208 VAC → 120 VACExample: 24 VAC → 96 VAC

Sanity check: fewer secondary turns → lower secondary voltage. More secondary turns → higher secondary voltage.

7. Rating language: volts, amperes, volt-amperes

  • Voltage rating: the voltage the winding is designed to use.
  • Current rating: how much current the winding can safely carry.
  • Volt-amperes (VA): the apparent power capacity, VA = V × I.

If a transformer is too small, control voltage can sag, contactors may chatter, and components can overheat.

8. Why not put every control device on line voltage?

Line voltage may be higher than the voltage rating of pushbuttons, pilot lights, relays, or control devices. A standard control voltage makes wiring and troubleshooting more consistent across machines.

Lower control voltage can reduce risk, but it is not harmless — treat every powered control circuit as hazardous until proven otherwise.

9. Turns ratio

Turns ratio compares how many turns are on the primary winding to how many turns are on the secondary winding.

TR = primary turns ÷ secondary turns

Example: primary = 100 turns, secondary = 50 turns → TR = 100 ÷ 50 = 2, written 2:1. A 2:1 turns ratio means the primary has twice as many turns as the secondary; in an ideal transformer, the secondary voltage will be half of the primary voltage.

10. Secondary voltage formula

secondary voltage = primary voltage ÷ turns ratio

Example: primary voltage = 120 VAC, turns ratio = 2 → secondary voltage = 120 ÷ 2 = 60 VAC.

Check the answer: because the turns ratio is greater than 1, this must be a step-down transformer — a 60 VAC answer makes sense. The voltmeter is on the secondary, so the calculated value is the voltage across the secondary terminals.

11. Worked examples

Worked Example 1 — Step-down: primary turns = 180, secondary turns = 45, primary voltage = 240 VAC.

  • Turns ratio = 180 ÷ 45 = 4:1
  • Secondary voltage = 240 ÷ 4 = 60 VAC
  • Step-down

Worked Example 2 — Step-up: primary turns = 50, secondary turns = 200, primary voltage = 24 VAC.

  • Turns ratio = 50 ÷ 200 = 0.25
  • Secondary voltage = 24 ÷ 0.25 = 96 VAC
  • Step-up — when the turns ratio is less than 1, the secondary has more turns than the primary, so the secondary voltage is higher.

12. Trainer transformer wiring

Primary taps: 208, 277, or 380 VAC. Secondary windings can be connected in series (240 VAC) or in parallel (120 VAC). H = high-side primary reference; X = low-side secondary reference.

Primary tap selection

Supply voltageConnect primary to terminals
208 VAC2 and 3
277 VAC2 and 4
380 VAC2 and 6

The primary tap must match the supply voltage — using the wrong tap can produce the wrong secondary voltage and can overheat or damage the transformer. Class 3 lab uses the 208 VAC primary tap.

Secondary connections: series vs. parallel

ConnectionLinksOutput across 2–5
SeriesLink terminal 3 to terminal 4240 VAC
ParallelLink terminal 2 to 3, and terminal 4 to 5120 VAC

Lab needs about 120 VAC control power, so the secondary windings must be connected in parallel. The links decide whether the two secondary windings add voltage or share current — the wrong link pattern produces the wrong output voltage. Always confirm link placement before energizing; do not wire from memory.

13. Primary and secondary protection

  • Primary-side protection: protects the transformer primary winding and upstream wiring from fault current.
  • Secondary-side protection: protects lower-voltage control conductors and devices from fault current.

A fuse or breaker is not there to make a circuit work normally — it opens when current becomes unsafe. The Amatrol schematic shows primary fuses feeding the control transformer; overcurrent protection is tied to National Electrical Code requirements.

14. Grounding one side of the secondary

In this lab, terminal 5 on the transformer secondary is connected to earth ground, giving one side of the control circuit a stable reference to ground. A fault from the ungrounded side to grounded metal becomes a high-current fault path, so protection can open. A floating secondary can create confusing voltage-to-ground readings.

15. Expected meter readings

MeasurementExpected readingWhy
Secondary terminals 2 to 5~120 VACFull secondary output
Terminal 5 to ground~0 VACTerminal 5 is grounded
Terminal 2 to ground~120 VACTerminal 2 is the ungrounded side

If 2-to-5 is not near 120 VAC, do not assume the transformer is bad. First check the primary tap, secondary links, fuse path, and meter setting.

16. Measuring control voltage & Live-Dead-Live

Use the digital multimeter on AC voltage. Black lead goes to COM (common); red lead goes to volts/ohms. Measure across secondary terminals 2 and 5. Use one hand where practical and keep your body out of the current path — this reduces the chance that a shock path goes across your chest from one hand to the other.

Proving de-energized — Live-Dead-Live:

  1. Live — prove the meter works on a known live source.
  2. Dead — measure the circuit you intend to touch; expect zero volts.
  3. Live — re-prove the meter on the known live source.

Re-checking proves a meter lead or internal connection didn't fail during testing — do not treat a zero-volt reading as trustworthy until the meter has been proven before and after.

17. Lab preview & self-check

What you will wire: connect the primary for the 208 VAC tap; configure the secondary for 120 VAC (parallel); connect terminal 5 to earth ground; use the selector switch (SS1) to turn the yellow lamp on and off; measure the secondary output across terminals 2 and 5.

Team self-check before energizing:

  • Every required green ground lead is connected to a dedicated ground bus point.
  • The primary tap matches the actual supply voltage.
  • The secondary links match the 120 VAC configuration.
  • Terminal 5 on the secondary is connected to earth ground.
  • The selector switch is in the center/off position before power is applied.

Measuring 2 to 5 should read about 120 VAC. If the lamp works but the voltage is wrong, do not skip the measurement — the lamp proves circuit action, the meter proves transformer output.

Retrieval practice

Try answering without looking, then reveal. For an interactive version, use the Quiz page.

1. What are the three jobs of a control transformer?

Step voltage to a useful control level, provide isolation between source and control circuit, and create a predictable measurement point for troubleshooting.

2. What is the formula for turns ratio?

TR = primary turns ÷ secondary turns.

3. What is the formula for secondary voltage?

Secondary voltage = primary voltage ÷ turns ratio.

4. Primary turns = 180, secondary turns = 45. What is the turns ratio?

180 ÷ 45 = 4:1.

5. Primary voltage = 240 VAC, turns ratio = 4:1. What is the secondary voltage?

240 ÷ 4 = 60 VAC.

6. Primary turns = 50, secondary turns = 200. Is this step-up or step-down?

Step-up — turns ratio = 0.25, which is less than 1, so the secondary has more turns and higher voltage.

7. What supply voltage uses primary terminals 2 and 3?

208 VAC.

8. Which secondary link pattern gives 120 VAC?

Parallel: link terminal 2 to 3, and terminal 4 to 5.

9. Which secondary link pattern gives 240 VAC?

Series: link terminal 3 to terminal 4.

10. What is the expected reading across secondary terminals 2 and 5 in this lab?

About 120 VAC.

11. Why is terminal 5 grounded?

It gives the control circuit a stable reference to ground and helps fault current clear protection.

12. What should terminal 5 to ground read, and why?

About 0 VAC, because terminal 5 is the grounded secondary terminal.

13. What should terminal 2 to ground read, and why?

About 120 VAC, because terminal 2 is the ungrounded side.

14. If 2-to-5 doesn't read near 120 VAC, what should you check first?

The primary tap, secondary links, fuse path, and meter setting — not assume the transformer is bad.

15. What are the three steps of Live-Dead-Live?

1) Prove the meter on a known live source. 2) Measure the circuit you intend to touch (expect zero volts). 3) Re-prove the meter on the known live source.

16. Why is the last "Live" step in Live-Dead-Live important?

A meter lead or internal connection can fail during testing; re-checking proves the zero-volt reading was real.

17. What is VA and how is it calculated?

Volt-amperes, apparent power. VA = V × I.

18. What can happen if a control transformer is undersized for its load?

Control voltage can sag, contactors may chatter, and components can overheat.

19. Why don't we feed every control device directly from line voltage?

Line voltage may exceed the voltage rating of control devices; a standard control voltage keeps wiring/troubleshooting consistent across machines.

20. What is the difference between primary-side and secondary-side protection?

Primary-side protects the transformer primary winding and upstream wiring; secondary-side protects the lower-voltage control conductors and devices.

21. In the lab, what does the selector switch (SS1) control?

The yellow lamp, powered by the transformer's secondary (control) voltage.

22. If the lamp turns on/off correctly but the voltage reading is wrong, what should you conclude?

Do not skip the measurement — the lamp only proves circuit action, not that the transformer output voltage is correct.

23. Why should the selector switch be in center/off before power is applied?

So the circuit starts in a known, safe state before energizing.

24. Primary = 24 VAC, secondary turns = 200, primary turns = 50. What is the secondary voltage?

Turns ratio = 50 ÷ 200 = 0.25; secondary voltage = 24 ÷ 0.25 = 96 VAC.

25. What topic comes next after control transformers?

Ladder logic — rails, rungs, normally open/closed contacts, coils, and device tags, powered by the control transformer studied this week.

Before the next class

  • Be able to calculate turns ratio and secondary voltage from either primary/secondary turns or a given ratio, without hesitating.
  • Know the primary tap table (208 → 2,3 · 277 → 2,4 · 380 → 2,6) and the series/parallel secondary link patterns.
  • Be able to state, without looking, the three expected meter readings (2–5, 5-to-ground, 2-to-ground) and why each value makes sense.
  • Practice explaining Live-Dead-Live out loud — a common exam trap is skipping the final "Live" re-check.
  • Review why isolation matters — a common trap is treating "lower voltage" as automatically "safe."
