Week 5 — Control Relays and Motor Starters

Sources: Class 5 POST-slides, Class 5 homework & answer key, Lab 5 (Amatrol Figure 5-2, three-wire motor starter)

Teaching focus: contactor action, overload protection, two-wire control, three-wire control, and seal-in operation.

Key takeaways

  1. The control circuit gives permission; the power circuit carries the energy.
  2. A control relay is a logic device; a contactor is a motor-duty power-switching device.
  3. Two-wire control uses a maintained command. Three-wire control uses momentary START/STOP plus seal-in memory.
  4. The seal-in sequence: START closes → coil energizes → F auxiliary closes → START can be released → STOP opens → coil drops out.
  5. STOP (and the overload control contact) are in series with the entire START/seal-in branch, so either one always removes power from the coil — "stop dominance."
  6. Overload elements (power side) sense current; the overload control contact (control side, NC) is what actually opens the coil circuit on a trip.

1. What you must be able to do

  • Explain the difference between the power circuit and the control circuit in a motor starter.
  • Distinguish a control relay (logic device) from a contactor (motor-duty power-switching device).
  • Read Normally Open (NO) and Normally Closed (NC) contacts from their normal, unactuated state.
  • Explain two-wire (maintained) control vs. three-wire (momentary START/STOP + seal-in) control.
  • Trace the full seal-in sequence, step by step, without guessing.
  • Explain why three-wire control does not automatically restart the motor after a power loss.
  • Distinguish overload elements (power side) from the overload control contact (control side).

2. Motor starter: two circuits working together

  • Power circuit: carries three-phase current from the line, through the F main contacts and the overload elements, to the motor (T1, T2, T3).
  • Control circuit: lower-current logic path — STOP, START, coil, auxiliary contact, and the overload control contact — that decides whether the power circuit should be closed.

Teaching point: the control circuit gives permission; the power circuit carries the energy.

3. Contactors and control relays: same idea, different duty

  • A control relay is a logic device — it switches low-current control signals.
  • A contactor is a motor-duty power-switching device — built to make and break the higher current the motor draws.

Key distinction: relay = logic device; contactor = motor-duty power-switching device. A motor starter is a contactor plus overload protection.

4. Normally Open and Normally Closed contacts (recap)

  • Normally Open (NO): open in the normal, unactuated state.
  • Normally Closed (NC): closed in the normal, unactuated state.
  • The symbol shows the normal state; actual state depends on whether the device is actuated.

Example: a Normally Closed STOP pushbutton is drawn closed. When the operator presses STOP, the physical contact opens and breaks the control path.

5. What the coil does

  • Coil de-energized: the F main contacts are open, and any F auxiliary contacts are in their normal state.
  • Coil energized: the F main contacts close (motor power applied), and the F auxiliary contact(s) switch to their actuated state.

Why it matters: every contact labeled "F" — main or auxiliary — moves together whenever the F coil changes state. Trace the coil first, then every contact that shares its name.

6. Two-wire control: maintained command

  • Two-wire control uses a maintained command — for example, a selector switch or float switch that stays in its actuated position.
  • As long as the maintained input is closed, the coil stays energized; no seal-in path is needed.

Classroom warning: two-wire control will restart the motor automatically as soon as the maintained input recloses — including after a power interruption.

Two-wire control uses a maintained command. Three-wire control uses momentary START/STOP plus seal-in memory.

7. Three-wire control: momentary START and STOP

Three-wire control uses momentary pushbuttons (they spring back when released) plus a seal-in auxiliary contact for memory:

  • STOP path: a Normally Closed pushbutton, wired first in the series path from L1.
  • START path: a Normally Open pushbutton, wired after STOP, in parallel with the seal-in path.
  • Seal-in path: the F auxiliary Normally Open contact, wired in parallel with START.

8. Seal-in sequence: what you must be able to explain, step by step

  1. START is pressed. The START contact closes and energizes the F coil.
  2. F coil energizes. The F main contacts close and motor power is applied.
  3. F auxiliary closes. The auxiliary contact creates a parallel holding path around START.
  4. START is released. The motor keeps running because current flows through the F auxiliary contact instead of through START.
  5. STOP is pressed. The STOP contact opens; the F coil drops out and all F contacts (main and auxiliary) return to normal.

Reasoning pattern for any seal-in question: identify the relevant device, determine whether it opens or closes, then trace whether the coil can remain energized.

9. Stop dominance and the overload contact

  • Stop dominance: STOP is wired ahead of the START/seal-in parallel pair, in series with the entire rung. Pressing STOP always breaks the path to the coil, even if START is also being held.
  • Overload control contact: also wired in series with the coil (alongside STOP), so an overload trip has the same fail-safe effect as pressing STOP.

10. Power-loss behavior: why three-wire control is common

  • During power loss: the F coil de-energizes and the F auxiliary seal-in contact opens (returns to normal/open).
  • When power returns: START is no longer being pressed, and the auxiliary contact is open, so there is no complete path to the coil. The operator must press START again.

This is the main safety advantage of three-wire control over two-wire (maintained) control: no automatic, unattended restart.

11. Overload protection: power side and control side

  • O.L. means overload element.
  • The overload elements are in the motor power path (one per phase) and sense motor current.
  • The overload relay also has a Normally Closed control contact, wired in the control circuit in series with the coil.
  • When the overload trips (sustained overcurrent), that control contact opens and de-energizes the coil.
  • The contactor opens (F main contacts open) and the motor stops.

14. Common mistakes — and what makes them wrong

  • Confusing the overload element with the overload control contact. The element senses current on the power side; the contact (NC, control side) is what actually opens the coil circuit.
  • Forgetting stop dominance. STOP must be in series with the entire START/seal-in branch, not just in series with START alone — otherwise the seal-in path could bypass STOP.
  • Assuming two-wire and three-wire control behave the same after a power loss. Two-wire (maintained) control restarts automatically; three-wire control requires START to be pressed again.
  • Skipping the panel ground check because it is not drawn on the simplified ladder — Amatrol Figure 5-2 and real panels still require it.

15. Apply it: predict before you energize

  1. With START released and STOP released, should the F coil be energized? → No. Until START has been pressed at least once, the F auxiliary contact has never closed, and START itself is open.
  2. When START is pressed, what path energizes the F coil? → Control transformer → STOP (NC, closed) → START (NO, now closed) → F coil → overload contact (NC, closed) → transformer return.
  3. After START is released, what keeps the F coil energized? → The F auxiliary contact, now closed (sealed in) in parallel with START, keeps the current path complete.
  4. What opens when STOP is pressed? → The STOP contact (NC) opens, breaking the series path to the coil regardless of the state of START or the F auxiliary contact.

16. Exit-ticket reasoning questions

  1. Why does the motor stay on after the START pushbutton is released?
  2. What opens when STOP is pressed, and what happens to the coil?
  3. Why does three-wire control avoid automatic restart after a power loss?
  4. What is the difference between the overload elements and the overload control contact?
  5. If the contactor pulls in but the motor does not run, would you first suspect the control circuit or the power circuit? Explain.

Answer by tracing the circuit path, not by guessing component names.

Retrieval practice

Try answering without looking, then reveal. For an interactive version, use the Quiz page.

1. What does the control circuit do vs. the power circuit?

The control circuit decides whether the contactor should close (gives permission); the power circuit carries the energy to the motor.

2. What is the key distinction between a control relay and a contactor?

A control relay is a logic device (low-current switching); a contactor is a motor-duty power-switching device built for higher current.

3. What is the normal state of a Normally Closed STOP pushbutton?

Closed — pressing STOP opens the physical contact and breaks the control path.

4. When the F coil energizes, what happens to the F main contacts and F auxiliary contacts?

They all move together: the F main contacts close (motor power applied) and the F auxiliary contact(s) switch to their actuated state.

5. What kind of command does two-wire control use?

A maintained command (e.g., a selector or float switch) that stays in its actuated position — no seal-in path is needed.

6. What is the main safety drawback of two-wire (maintained) control?

It restarts the motor automatically as soon as the maintained input recloses, including after a power interruption.

7. In three-wire control, where is STOP wired relative to START and the seal-in contact?

STOP (NC) is wired first, in series with the entire START/seal-in parallel pair.

8. In three-wire control, what is wired in parallel with START?

The F auxiliary Normally Open contact (the seal-in path).

9. What is the first step in the seal-in sequence?

START is pressed, closing the START contact and energizing the F coil.

10. After START is pressed and the F coil energizes, what happens next in the sequence?

The F main contacts close (motor power applied), and then the F auxiliary contact closes, creating a parallel holding path around START.

11. Why does the motor keep running after START is released?

Current flows through the now-closed F auxiliary (seal-in) contact instead of through START.

12. What happens when STOP is pressed?

The STOP contact opens, the F coil drops out, and all F contacts (main and auxiliary) return to normal.

13. What is "stop dominance"?

STOP is in series with the entire rung, so pressing STOP always breaks the path to the coil, even if START is being held at the same time.

14. Where is the overload control contact wired, and what type of contact is it?

In series with the coil (alongside STOP); it is Normally Closed.

15. What happens to the F auxiliary contact during a power loss?

The F coil de-energizes and the F auxiliary seal-in contact opens (returns to normal/open).

16. Why doesn't the motor restart automatically when power returns after a loss, in three-wire control?

START is no longer being pressed and the auxiliary contact is open, so there is no complete path to the coil — START must be pressed again.

17. What is the difference between overload elements and the overload control contact?

Overload elements are power-side current sensors (one per phase); the overload control contact is the control-side NC switch that opens the coil circuit when a trip is detected.

18. What happens to the motor when the overload control contact opens on a trip?

The coil de-energizes, the F main contacts open, and the motor stops.

19. In Amatrol Figure 5-2, what does the top section of the diagram show?

The three-phase motor power path: F main contacts, overload elements, and T1-T3.

20. Why must panel grounds be verified even if not shown on the simplified ladder diagram?

Panel grounds are still required on the physical trainer/real panel; the simplified ladder is a teaching aid, not the full wiring authority.

21. With START and STOP both released, should the F coil be energized?

No — START is open, and until START has been pressed at least once, the F auxiliary seal-in contact has never closed either.

22. If the contactor pulls in but the motor does not run, should you first suspect the control circuit or the power circuit?

The power circuit — the control circuit has already done its job (the coil pulled in), so the fault is more likely downstream, in the power path to the motor.

23. What does Class 6 add on top of this week's three-wire starter concepts?

Additional control functions, such as jogging, multiple START/STOP stations, or interlocking, layered on top of the seal-in circuit.
